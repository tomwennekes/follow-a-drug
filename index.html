    Real case The dentist's injection does not work

    A patient has an abscess: an infected, inflamed spot where the tissue is more acidic, about pH 6.5 instead of 7.4. The dentist injects lidocaine, but the tooth stays painful. To block the nerve, lidocaine must first cross the nerve membrane in its neutral form.

    Which residue holds the N⁺?

    Every hint in the hubs works this way: a nudge first, then a step, then the full answer.

    • Ionic: N⁺ ··· Asp147 ⊖, 4.5 Å
    • Cation–π: the N⁺ over the ring of Tyr529, 4.0 Å
    • H-bonds: the ester C=O and the OH with Asn507, 2.5 and 3.0 Å

    Tiotropium stays on the M3 receptor for about two days. Without its OH that falls to about 49 minutes, about 56 times shorter: the OH–Asn507 H-bond holds it in place (Tautermann et al., J Med Chem 2013).

    PDB 4U15: rat M3 receptor, X-ray 2.8 Å; the 17 residues within 4.5 Å are the same in human M3. One frozen snapshot, provisional; distances rounded to 0.5 Å; H atoms not shown. As in the Drug Journey.

    What you just did

    The main idea: a drug's charge depends on the pH around it, and mainly the neutral form crosses a membrane. That is why the same molecule behaves differently in the stomach, in the blood or in an abscess. Tiotropium is the exception: with no proton to lose, it stays charged at every pH. And once a drug reaches its target, how tightly it holds decides how long it works. On the way you used the four ways the hubs teach:

    • Turn it in 3DIn step 1 you turned the molecule to find the atom that matters; with tiotropium, step 4 turned the receptor around it. Shape decides what a molecule does.
    • Change it, see itIn steps 2 and 3 one pH slider moved the curve and the molecules at once.
    • Predict, then checkYou chose neutral or ionised first, guessed how much reaches the nerve in the case and how much water one puff needs; then the model showed why.
    • Stuck? Ask for a hintThe hints in every step go one step at a time, up to the full answer.
